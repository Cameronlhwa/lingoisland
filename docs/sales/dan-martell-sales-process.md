# Dan Martell — "Sell anything to anyone" (sales process notes)

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nsFKkeQg-V0
Saved: 2026-11-09

## Core mindset
- Selling = serving. You're helping someone make a decision (cut off the bad option), not talking them into something.
- People love to buy; they hate being sold to. Neediness ("commission breath") pushes people onto their heels.
- Ask yourself every time: *can I actually help this person?* If no, give them their time back. If yes, it's your responsibility to help them decide.
- Pros follow a process; amateurs freestyle. "If you don't have a process for selling, the prospect will show you their process for not buying."
- A repeatable process makes failure diagnosable — you can see which step broke.

## The 4-step process (never skip, never jump ahead)

### 1. Connect — build trust, control the frame
- Don't open by talking about yourself/your company (the "bad first date").
- Trust = respecting their time, asking smart questions, showing you understand their situation.
- Frame: you're the expert/doctor, they have a problem, your job is to see if it's a fit. Set the agenda: "If it's not a fit, I'll tell you."
- Two opening questions:
  - **Why us / why me?** — they sell you on why they chose you.
  - **Why now?** — what's true right now that makes this a priority. They sell themselves on urgency.

### 2. Diagnose — find the problem behind the problem
- What people say is the problem usually isn't the real one. Solve the surface problem and they won't believe you understand them.
- Doctor story: the pain was in the wrist, the real injury was the knuckle. Explaining someone's pain better than they can = you become the expert.
- **The sale happens in the gap** between current reality and desired outcome. Stretch the gap:
  1. Future first (easier to talk about): "12 months from now we're celebrating — what happened?"
  2. Then current reality, measured against that goal ("you want 12 weeks of travel — how many this year?").
  3. What's in the way?
  4. **What have you tried before?** — critical. If you skip it, they'll assume your solution is like what already failed, and you'll be fighting an objection you can't see.
- Buying zone: bring the overconfident down a peg ("sounds like you have a plan — why haven't you hit it yet?") and lift the uncertain up ("my best clients felt exactly the same when they started").
- Write down every roadblock — you'll use them in Prescribe.

### 3. Prescribe — map the solution to *their* roadblocks only
- No prescription without diagnosis (that's malpractice). Painkillers sell better than vitamins.
- Play it back first: "You're here, you want to be there, this is in the way — did I get that right?" (3–4 items).
- Hand over control: "I've asked a lot of questions — where do you want to go from here?" They usually ask you to show them.
- Don't dump every feature. A confused mind never buys. Only mention benefits that map to the roadblocks they agreed to — "tailored suit" feel.
- Price delivery: state the price, then immediately add one more "ninja" benefit tied to a struggle people like them have, then ask. The first to speak after the *offer* (price + benefit + ask) loses.
- **Clear common obstacles BEFORE prescribing** so they become obstacles, not objections:
  1. Who else is involved in the decision?
  2. Money — have you set a budget for solving this?
  3. Time — how much time per week could you give this?
  Their own answers become your ammunition later.

### 4. Commit — collect money, not promises
- A yes isn't a sale until payment. Collect on the call (deposit, card, wire — some real commitment).
- Never just "send a proposal" — schedule a time to walk through it together.
- Assumptive close: "Which card do you want to put that on?" Expect an objection, acknowledge ("totally makes sense"), handle it, ask again.
- If it can't close today, "bam bam": book the next call on the spot, then ask a favor — "if you can't make it, let me know ahead of time." The small commitment triggers consistency bias.

## Review your game tape (the improvement loop)
- Every lost sale leaves clues. Amateurs move to the next call; pros review (Kobe watching first-half tape at halftime).
- Collect transcripts of wins AND losses — not knowing why you win is as bad as not knowing why you lose.
- Run them through AI with four questions:
  1. Connect — did I build trust and control the frame?
  2. Diagnose — did I find real pain and create desire for a solution?
  3. Prescribe — was the solution as clear and simple as possible?
  4. Commit — did I ask for the money with confidence?
- Ask for patterns, advice, and improved scripts. This becomes a self-improving sales manager.
